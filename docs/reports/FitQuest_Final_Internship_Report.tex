\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

Interactive exercise systems typically sense through one modality. Camera-based
systems observe posture but cannot see grip rotation or measure effort;
wearable systems measure the limb they are attached to but have no view of the
body or of the weight being lifted. Each is limited in ways the other could
cover, yet combining them raises a problem of its own: the streams run at
different rates, arrive over different transports, and fail independently.

FitQuest addresses that combination directly. It is a middleware layer that
converts camera-derived body pose and object detection, inertial telemetry from
a purpose-built forearm unit, and physiological context from a commercial
smartwatch into a single normalized, versioned signal specification. A browser
application consumes that specification --- and nothing else --- to prescribe
exercises, validate repetitions against multi-sensor evidence, and provide live
feedback, demonstrating the complete path from physical movement to interaction.

Two design properties carry most of the contribution. Per-session calibration
records each user's own range of motion, so comparable physical effort maps to
comparable normalized values without retraining or per-user code. And the
evidence model is deliberately asymmetric: a small set of postural conditions
can reject a repetition, while every sensor-derived signal can only corroborate
it. That asymmetry was not designed in advance; it came from a real session in
which a single briefly stale sensor silently stopped the count.

The system was developed over a twelve-week Mitacs Globalink Research
Internship at the Persuasive Computing Lab, Dalhousie University. It has been
validated technically --- 62 automated tests, five recorded capture sessions,
and full sessions with all three modalities operating concurrently --- but not
with independent participants. The report states throughout what that does and
does not license anyone to claim.

\vspace{0.4cm}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} sensor fusion; human--computer interaction;
exercise interaction; inertial measurement; pose estimation; wearable
computing; per-user calibration; exergames.

\section{Introduction}

The Persuasive Computing Lab at Dalhousie University studies systems intended to
influence health and well-being behaviour. This internship was framed around
developing a system for monitoring health and well-being, with the specific
proposal being \emph{IronQuest 3D --- Smart Dumbbell Fitness Gaming Platform}:
sensor-enabled dumbbells feeding a browser-based 3D game, with repetition,
tempo, and range-of-motion signals supporting feedback, goal setting, analytics,
and exercise adherence.

Two constraints shaped what followed. The internship had a twelve-week maximum
from a May 18, 2026 start, and the expected output was due August 7, 2026 ---
approximately eleven weeks in. A fitness gaming platform is not a research
contribution on that timeline; a validated sensing method can be. The project
was therefore renarrowed on June 23, 2026 to \emph{FitQuest}, a sensor-fusion
system that converts camera, inertial, and physiological measurements into a
normalized signal specification, with a browser application built on top of it
to demonstrate the complete path from physical movement to interaction.
Section~\ref{sec:scope} sets out that decision and its reasoning in full.

The primary deliverable is the middleware layer rather than the application
built upon it. The system publishes a documented, per-user calibrated payload
that downstream applications consume without direct knowledge of the underlying
vision models, inertial hardware, or wireless transports.

\subsection{Research questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

The internship did not begin with formal research questions; the pivot in week
six (Section~\ref{sec:scope}) produced them. Stated in the form the work
actually addressed:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.4cm,style=nextline,itemsep=4pt]
\item[RQ1] Can camera, inertial, and wearable streams --- running at different
and independent rates, over different transports --- be combined into a single
signal specification stable enough to drive a real-time interactive
application?

\item[RQ2] Can a short per-session calibration make those signals comparable
across users of differing body proportion and mobility, without retraining a
model or writing per-user code?

\item[RQ3] What evidence model does a multi-sensor system need so that a
missing or delayed sensor degrades the interaction rather than halting it?

\item[RQ4] What can a single 2D camera not resolve about dumbbell exercise
that an inertial unit on the forearm can, and how much of that potential is
realizable without a calibrated sensor-to-segment mapping?
\end{description}

This report answers RQ1 and RQ3 with a working system and the design reasoning
behind it. RQ2 is answered by construction but \emph{not} by measurement: the
calibration mechanism exists and behaves as intended, but no participant study
has tested whether it actually produces comparable values across people.
Section~\ref{sec:studyreqs} sets out what such a study would require. RQ4 has a
partial and negative-leaning answer that Section~\ref{sec:forearm} reports in
full.

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An asynchronous multi-modal middleware} that fuses camera-derived
pose and object context, forearm inertial telemetry, and wearable physiological
context by latest-value sampling with explicit per-sensor freshness metadata,
and that continues to operate when any single stream is absent.

\item \textbf{A versioned, documented signal specification} --- \code{game\_control},
schema \code{2026-\allowbreak 08-\allowbreak fusion-\allowbreak v2} --- that
decouples interactive clients from sensor- and model-specific formats, together
with a flat per-frame export format intended for offline analysis.

\item \textbf{A session-adaptive calibration and temporal filtering pipeline}
that converts raw observations into normalized exercise-control signals, with
an explicit quality assessment that reports when a calibration window produced
a range too narrow to be useful.

\item \textbf{An evidence model for multi-sensor validation} that separates
postural preconditions, which may reject an event, from sensor corroboration,
which may only strengthen confidence --- derived from observed failure rather
than assumed.

\item \textbf{A browser-based proof of concept} implementing ten prescribed
dumbbell exercises with per-repetition validation, graceful sensor degradation,
and fully local operation.

\item \textbf{An instrumented hardware prototype}: an ESP32-S3 and BNO08x
forearm unit with a printed enclosure, publishing over USB serial and Wi-Fi UDP
concurrently, with a self-recovering discovery protocol for changing networks.
\end{enumerate}

What the work does \emph{not} contribute is equally worth stating: no
participant evaluation, no validated biomechanical measurement, and no
demonstrated behaviour change. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} is explicit about
each.

\section{Related Approaches}
\label{sec:related}

% NOTE FOR PAPER EXTRACTION: this section is written around approach families
% rather than around individual citations, because only the lab's own work was
% verified against publisher records during the internship. Before submission,
% each family below needs representative citations from the literature.

Interactive exercise systems sense through one of three broad arrangements.
Each resolves some ambiguities and leaves others open, and the pattern of what
each cannot see motivates the arrangement used here.

\subsection{Camera-based sensing}

A camera observes the whole body at once. Pose estimation yields joint positions
without instrumenting the user, which makes setup trivial and makes the system
usable by anyone who walks into frame. Object detection additionally identifies
what the user is holding, which no body-worn sensor can do.

Three limitations recur. A single camera produces a 2D projection, so depth is
inferred from apparent scale rather than measured, and movements differing
mainly in depth are poorly separated. Self-occlusion is routine rather than
exceptional: in dumbbell exercise the working arm passes in front of the torso
on most repetitions. And rotation about a limb's own axis --- the difference
between a curl and a hammer curl --- produces almost no change in joint
positions, so it is effectively invisible.

\subsection{Body-worn inertial sensing}

An inertial unit measures the segment it is attached to directly, at rates far
above camera frame rate, and is unaffected by occlusion or lighting. Rotation
about the limb axis, invisible to a camera, is exactly what a gyroscope
measures well.

Its limitations are the complement. A unit knows its own motion and nothing
about the rest of the body, so posture must be inferred or assumed. It cannot
identify the object being held. Orientation expressed in the device frame is
not a joint angle until a sensor-to-segment calibration relates the two, and
that step is frequently skipped --- Section~\ref{sec:forearm} reports what
skipping it cost here. Magnetometer-free fusion avoids magnetic disturbance at
the price of a drifting, relative heading.

\subsection{Commercial wearables}

Consumer smartwatches provide physiological context, principally heart rate,
with no assembly and with the reliability of a shipped product. This is the
arrangement the host lab has used across its recent
work~\cite{fitpulse,calmme}, and it is a sound default: the device is
maintained by its vendor and worn by participants who already own one.

The constraint is that access is bounded by what a vendor exposes. Sampling
rate, available channels, and permitted transports are set by the platform, not
by the study. Wrist-based optical heart rate also degrades during movement
involving the forearm, which is the condition an upper-body exercise
application creates.

\subsection{Where this work sits}

The three arrangements fail in different places, which is the argument for
combining them: the camera supplies body context and object identity, the
inertial unit supplies limb-axis rotation and occlusion-immune motion, and the
wearable supplies physiological context. Combining them raises its own problem
--- the streams run at different rates, over different transports, and fail
independently --- and that problem, rather than any single modality, is what
this work addresses.

Two further distinctions are worth naming. Most exercise-recognition systems
classify activity from labelled sensor data; this one derives geometric signals
and calibrates them per user, which trades the ability to recognize unforeseen
movements for interpretability and for not needing a training set per exercise.
And most such systems are built as a single application; here the sensing layer
and the application are separated by an explicit versioned specification, so
the sensing work is reusable by a different application without modification.

\section{Change of Scope}
\label{sec:scope}

\subsection{Rationale}

By the third week of the internship it became apparent that the original scope
contained a sequential dependency chain that could not be completed and validated
within the available time:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Instrumented dumbbells require designing, building, and calibrating custom
      hardware for each weight.
\item A 3D game platform requires game design, asset production, and interaction
      tuning.
\item Adherence analytics requires longitudinal data from participants, which
      depends on the first two components being operational.
\end{enumerate}

Pursuing all three in parallel would have produced three incomplete components.
The contribution with the clearest research value is the intermediate layer: the
proposition that heterogeneous, asymmetrically mounted sensors can be combined
into a single per-user calibrated specification stable enough to drive a
real-time interactive application.

\subsection{Revised scope, June 23, 2026}

The project was formally renarrowed on June 23, 2026. The pivot report recorded
five decisions:

\begin{itemize}
\item The final game is deferred and reduced to a proof-of-concept slice.
\item The vision system is fixed at body pose and dumbbell/weight detection. No
      additional vision model enters scope.
\item The abandoned keypoint-analysis runtime path is removed in full, including
      code, command-line interface entries, tests, tools, model runs, and
      documentation.
\item \code{game\_control} is reworked from a game-input structure into a general
      sensor-fusion payload.
\item Per-user auto-calibration becomes a requirement rather than a refinement.
\end{itemize}

The calibration decision is the one with the greatest bearing on the research
value of the work. Without calibration, every normalized signal encodes the body
proportions and range of motion of the individual who developed it. With
calibration, comparable physical effort maps to comparable normalized values
across users, which is the property required for the specification to transfer.

\subsection{Non-goals}

The following were declared out of scope in writing and remained so:
complex game development, multiplayer functionality, production platform
features, rule-based repetition counting from fixed joint-angle thresholds, and
any vision model beyond the two named above.

Two of these deserve comment, because their status is the substance of the
work rather than a caveat on it.

G2 is the goal with research content and the one the internship could not
close. The mechanism exists and behaves as designed: bounds are recorded per
side, normalization uses them, and a window that captured too narrow a range is
reported as such rather than silently producing a saturation-prone signal. What
is absent is evidence that two different people performing comparable effort

participants, and Section~\ref{sec:studyreqs} states what testing it would take.
Until then the correct description is a mechanism designed for
transferability, not a demonstrated transferable measurement.

G4 is met in the sense that the system is usable at interaction rate and that
three specific latency sources were identified and removed --- a capture buffer
returning stale frames, an oversampled preview, and per-frame object inference
where strided inference suffices. It is unmeasured in the sense that no
end-to-end figure exists from physical movement to visible response. For an
interactive system that is a primary quality attribute, and its absence is a
gap rather than an oversight in reporting.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\subsection{Design principle}

The architecture follows one rule: clients consume \code{game\_control} and do
not access raw sensor output. The browser receives normalized scalars, booleans,
and categorical labels, not bounding boxes, quaternions, or Bluetooth
characteristics.

This separation has two consequences. A rehabilitation application, an adherence
monitor, or an alternative game can be developed against the same specification
without modifying the sensing code. It also makes the system testable: the 62
automated tests exercise the specification directly, with no camera or hardware
present.

\subsection{Frame-level data flow}

Each camera frame passes through the following stages:

\begin{lstlisting}
Camera frame
  -> YOLO26 body pose (17 COCO keypoints)
  -> temporal pose smoothing (blend and occlusion hold)
  -> dumbbell/weight detector (strided, with temporal tracking)
  -> body/object side association (wrist and forearm proximity)
  -> pose-stream state
  -> auto-calibrated motion signal analysis
  -> wearable context merge
  -> inertial telemetry merge
  -> game_control payload
  -> flat signal_log record
  -> OpenCV monitor / JSONL file / browser gateway
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Fusion model}
\label{sec:fusionmodel}

The three sensing streams run at different and independent rates: the camera
between roughly 8 and 18\,FPS depending on scene and hardware, the inertial unit
at approximately 15\,Hz, and the watch at one sample every 3\,s. The pipeline does
not resample, interpolate, align clocks, or compensate for drift. On each camera
frame it attaches the most recent available sample from each of the other two
streams, together with that sample's timestamp and age.

This is asynchronous late fusion by latest-value sampling with explicit freshness
metadata, not temporal synchronization. The choice keeps end-to-end latency at
camera rate and allows any stream to be absent without stalling the others, which
matters for an interactive application. Its consequences are equally explicit:
values combined into one payload may describe moments up to one wearable interval
apart, and a sample remains the latest until it exceeds its staleness threshold.
The inter-device timing error was not quantified during the internship. Heart
rate is therefore treated as slow context rather than as a frame-level control
signal, and the specification carries per-sensor age so that a consumer can apply
its own freshness policy.

In practice a mobile phone hotspot was used throughout, and it served two
distinct roles at once. It provided the shared subnet that lets the inertial
unit's UDP telemetry reach the laptop, and it provided the internet access that
the Connect IQ relay path requires. This is why the hotspot, rather than the
campus network, became the standard configuration: the campus network uses
enterprise authentication that the board's firmware does not implement, and it
does not reliably permit UDP broadcast between client devices.

The hotspot topology also caused the problem the relay was built to solve. When
the laptop is a hotspot client and the phone is simultaneously the gateway and
the Garmin Connect bridge, the phone does not route the watch's request back to
the laptop. Introducing an external HTTPS relay removed that dependency on
local routing, at the cost of requiring internet access.

Two configurations are therefore worth distinguishing:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline,itemsep=3pt]
\item[Fully offline configuration] Camera, inertial unit over USB serial, and
watch over BLE. No network of any kind is required. Heart rate is available but
the richer watch payload and wireless glove operation are not.
\item[Demonstrated configuration] Camera, inertial unit over Wi-Fi UDP, and
watch via the Connect IQ relay. Requires a shared subnet \emph{and} internet
access. This is the configuration used for the sessions reported here.
\end{description}

The dependency on an external relay for the richest wearable path is a
deployment limitation, not a design preference. A self-hosted relay on the same
subnet would remove the internet requirement while keeping the HTTPS endpoint
Connect IQ requires, and is noted as future work.

\subsection{Gateway implementation}

The browser gateway uses the Python standard library \code{http.server} rather
than a web framework. The client must run on a laptop without an additional
service, and, as Table~\ref{tab:network} records, without any external request
of its own. Server-Sent Events over HTTP are sufficient for a unidirectional
payload stream at camera frame rate, and MJPEG over
\code{multipart/x-mixed-replace} is sufficient for the preview image. A framework
would introduce a dependency and an additional failure mode without providing
capability the demonstration requires.

The preview stream is downscaled to 640\,px at JPEG quality 72. The browser
renders it in a card approximately 290\,px wide, so encoding at full camera
resolution represented roughly threefold oversampling, which added encode time on
the publishing thread and decode time in the browser and produced observable
latency between the user's movement and the displayed image. Detection continues
to operate on the full-resolution frame, so accuracy is unaffected.

\section{Sensing Subsystems}
\label{sec:sensing}

\subsection{Computer vision}

Two YOLO26 models~\cite{ultralytics} run per frame. The body-pose model produces
17 keypoints in the COCO topology~\cite{coco}; a separately trained detector
produces \code{dumbbell}, \code{weight}, and \code{other} boxes.

Three mechanisms address failure modes observed during testing:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline,itemsep=3pt]
\item[Pose smoothing] The pose model estimates each frame independently, so
lighting changes, motion blur, or partial occlusion can displace a wrist or elbow
for a single frame. Visible joints are blended with their recent position, and a
joint that disappears is held briefly before being excluded from analysis.
\item[Strided object inference] The dumbbell detector runs every $N$ frames
(default 2), with cached boxes reused between runs. Object position changes more
slowly than pose, so this reduces inference cost without measurable loss.
\item[Temporal object tracking] A detected box is retained for a configurable
number of missed frames and smoothed across frames. A dumbbell passing in front
of the torso is regularly lost for two or three frames; without this mechanism the
system would report the weight as released mid-repetition.
\end{description}

Accepted boxes must further satisfy per-class confidence floors, frame-area ratio
bounds, and a wrist-or-forearm proximity requirement before contributing to the
loaded state. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the measurements that motivate
this filtering.

\subsection{Wearable subsystem}

The Garmin Venu 3 provides physiological and secondary motion context. It is not
used as a primary motion sensor. Three access paths were implemented:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Connect IQ application.} A watch application transmits heart rate
and contact state, three-axis acceleration with derived magnitude, delta, and
motion state, battery level, and transmission metadata, at a 3\,s interval and
only while its view is active. The wearable schema on the Python and relay sides
additionally accepts RR intervals, gyroscope, and location fields, but the
current watch application does not produce them.
\item \textbf{Cloudflare Worker relay.} A serverless endpoint backed by a D1
database receives the watch payload and serves the most recent sample.
\item \textbf{Bluetooth Low Energy fallback.} Heart rate only, from the watch's
standard broadcast mode.
\end{enumerate}

The relay addresses a specific network condition. When the laptop is connected to
the phone hotspot, the phone acts simultaneously as hotspot gateway and as the
Garmin Connect bridge, and does not route the watch request back to a hotspot
client. Connect IQ additionally requires HTTPS. A persistent HTTPS relay resolves
both conditions and replaced an earlier approach based on ephemeral tunnels,
whose URL changed on each restart and required rebuilding and re-sideloading the
watch application.

The BLE fallback is disabled by default. Operating both sources against the same
wearable file allowed a BLE missing-device state to overwrite a valid Connect IQ
sample.

\subsection{Physical enclosure}

The inertial hardware was initially assembled on a breadboard with jumper wires,
which is adequate for desk testing but unsuitable for movement capture. A
protective enclosure was modelled in Blender and printed in two revisions. The
repository contains the Blender source, the exported STL, and sliced G-code for
the printer profile used.

The enclosure fixes the sensor orientation relative to the forearm. The rotation
at which the enclosure sits on the strap determines an absolute pitch offset;
Section~\ref{sec:browser} describes how repetition validation accommodates this.

\subsection{Power}
\label{sec:power}

Wireless operation is not simply a matter of selecting the Wi-Fi transport. The
board draws its power over USB, so running untethered from the laptop requires a
separate supply travelling with the user. Sessions to date have used a USB power
bank, carried in a pocket or clipped to clothing, with a cable running to the
forearm unit.

This works but leaves the arrangement incomplete in two respects. The cable is
still a tether, now to the user's own pocket rather than to the laptop, and it
remains a source of mechanical disturbance and of session interruption if it
pulls loose. And the supply was chosen for availability rather than fit: the
unit in use is rated at 20\,000\,mAh.

That figure is worth converting into what it means for the design, because it
sets the size of the mismatch. A cell rating of 20\,000\,mAh at a nominal
3.7\,V is roughly 74\,Wh; delivered at 5\,V through a converter of typical
efficiency, approximately 12{,}500\,mAh is available at the output. The board
transmits continuously over Wi-Fi, which dominates its consumption, and
datasheet-typical figures for a connected station sending small packets at this
cadence, plus the inertial sensor and the board's own regulator and indicators,
put the draw in the region of 150--200\,mA. That implies on the order of
sixty hours of continuous operation.

A session lasts tens of minutes. The supply is therefore oversized by roughly
two orders of magnitude, and the consequence is not merely inelegant: a pack of
this capacity weighs several hundred grams, and mass carried on or near the
limb being measured is a confound in the measurement rather than only a comfort
problem. A capacity of 1{,}000--1{,}500\,mAh would cover a full day of sessions
at a fraction of the size and mass.

These are estimates from datasheet-typical consumption, not measurements. The
distinction matters because the design decision they inform --- what capacity
an integrated supply should carry --- deserves a measured basis, and
Section~\ref{sec:studyreqs} lists the missing power budget among the
instrumentation gaps a participant study would first have to close.

The enclosure currently has no provision for carrying a supply. Section
\ref{sec:futurework} sets out the options --- a compartment sized for a small
power bank, or a battery holder integrated into the enclosure --- as the change
that would make the wireless path genuinely self-contained.

\subsection{Per-user calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}

At session start the system runs a calibration window, 7\,s by default, during
which the user moves through a comfortable range. Per-side minimum and maximum
bounds are recorded for each feature, and subsequent measurements are normalized
against those bounds. Comparable physical effort therefore maps to comparable
normalized values across users of differing height, limb length, and mobility,
without model retraining or per-user code changes.

Calibration reports a quality assessment of \code{good}, \code{limited}, or
\code{insufficient}. The timer and sample-count conditions can both be satisfied
while the user has moved very little, which produces a narrow span and a
saturation-prone signal for the remainder of the session. The browser's
calibration control monitors the reported quality rather than closing after a
fixed interval.

\subsection{Exercise disambiguation signals}

A curl and an overhead triceps extension bend the elbow through a comparable
range. Elbow angle alone does not separate them; the position of the upper arm
relative to the torso does. Two signal families address this:

\begin{itemize}
\item \code{upper\_arm\_angle}, derived from the hip--shoulder--elbow angle.
      $0^\circ$ corresponds to the arm at the side; $180^\circ$ to overhead.
\item \code{torso\_hinge}, derived from the angle between vertical and the line
      joining the hip midpoint to the shoulder midpoint. Near zero when upright,
      increasing with forward hinge.
\end{itemize}

Both are published in calibrated $[0,1]$ form and in raw degrees. The browser's
repetition gates use the raw degree values. Whether an arm is near the torso or
overhead is a fixed anatomical relationship and does not require per-user
calibration; further, an exercise that holds the upper arm still, such as a curl,
leaves the calibrated span narrow early in a session, which made the calibrated
form unstable enough to trigger falsely. The calibrated forms remain in
\code{signal\_log} for cross-user comparison.

\subsection{Asymmetric hardware configuration}

The system assumes by default that the inertial glove is worn on the right hand
and the watch on the left. Both are configurable from the command line. This
asymmetry reflects the expected deployment configuration and is handled
explicitly in the specification. It also produces a limitation: with a single
glove, a grip-rotation difference such as curl against hammer curl can be
verified only on the instrumented side.

\subsection{Session flow}

A session is a sequence of prescribed sets, not a free-form activity. The client
announces each exercise before its set begins, the way a coach would call out
the next movement, and counting is suspended while that announcement card is
showing.

That suspension caused a defect worth recording, because it illustrates how a
small state-machine error can be invisible in component testing and obvious in
use. The transition flag was set when the card appeared but never cleared when
the card auto-dismissed --- only a full session stop reset it. Repetitions
therefore counted normally for the first exercise of a session and for none of
the others. The timer that dismisses the card now clears the flag itself.

Before a session can start, both the inertial unit and the watch must be
reporting live data, re-checked each time the start control is pressed. There is
deliberately no override. The rationale is evidentiary rather than technical:
the middleware runs perfectly well without either device, but a session recorded
without them produces data that cannot be distinguished later from a session
where they silently failed. Requiring both at the start makes the recording
self-describing.

\subsection{The evidence model}
\label{sec:evidence}

Repetition validation separates three categories, and the separation is the
design decision that matters most in this section.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline,itemsep=4pt]
\item[Postural preconditions --- may reject] Conditions that must hold for a
repetition to count, evaluated from raw degree measurements: the upper arm
genuinely overhead for a press, the torso genuinely hinged for a row, the upper
arm near the torso for a curl. These exist because five of the ten exercises
drive counting from the same elbow-bend signal, and nothing else distinguishes
them. Without this, a triceps extension counts as a curl.

\item[Corroboration --- may only strengthen] Inertial motion observed during
the repetition, watch motion state, and grip orientation. These raise
confidence and feed a ``sensor-verified'' count reported alongside the raw
count, but cannot prevent a repetition from being awarded.

\item[Advisory --- may only inform] The forearm excursion check
(Section~\ref{sec:forearm}). It produces a message to the user and nothing else.
\end{description}

The asymmetry came from a real session rather than from design. In the first
implementation every sensor could veto, and one briefly stale sensor stopped
counting entirely with no indication of why --- the set sat at 0 of 8 for its
whole duration. Four separate corrections followed, and each generalizes beyond
this project:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A global early-return became a scoped one.} The alternation rule
tested whether both sides were in the bent phase, so a single mis-tracked arm
--- one session had the left arm pinned at an extension of 0.23 while it hung
motionless --- blocked \emph{both} arms indefinitely. It now fires only when
both sides entered that phase within 700\,ms of each other: genuinely
concurrent work, not merely concurrent state.

\item \textbf{Every latched state needs a release.} A side whose extension
dropped below the threshold and never recovered stayed bent forever. A 9-second
timeout, longer than any plausible repetition, releases it.

\item \textbf{A rule is only enforceable when its evidence is observable.} If
the opposite arm has shown no phase activity for 12 seconds it is untracked, and
the user may be alternating perfectly without the camera seeing it. Rejecting
repetitions then punishes correct form for a tracking failure, so an
unobservable partner disables the rule rather than blocking on it.

\item \textbf{A stale negative must expire.} Once a side was marked unloaded,
every subsequent unresolved frame returned that same negative, so the interface
reported the dumbbell out of view while the detector was reporting accepted
dumbbells in frame. A stale negative now reverts to unknown when the scene
shows dumbbells again.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Adaptive thresholds}

Repetition thresholds adapt to the range the user is actually producing rather
than to fixed values, because the same nominal movement spans very different
signal ranges between people and between exercises.

The first implementation tracked each side's lifetime minimum and maximum for
the current exercise and only ever widened. One unusually wide or unusually
shallow early repetition therefore skewed the threshold for the rest of the set;
in a real session this manifested as repetitions ceasing to count after the
first few, or requiring an exaggerated motion. The current implementation uses a
6-second rolling window, so the thresholds follow the recent range and forget an
outlier within seconds. Difficulty contributes a strictness parameter that
tightens how much of the observed span a repetition must traverse.

\subsection{Alternation as an enforced property}

Six of the ten exercises alternate arms. Until a real session exposed it, that
was a label rather than a rule: a repetition was awarded to any side completing
a down-up transition. Curling both arms together therefore scored two
repetitions from one movement, and eight consecutive repetitions on the same arm
completed an ``alternating'' set. Two rules now apply, subject to the
observability condition above: simultaneous work on both sides resolves no
repetition, and a resolving repetition must come from the side opposite the
previous one, with the first repetition of a set unconstrained.

\subsection{Occlusion tolerance}

A dumbbell passing behind the torso is lost by the detector for two or three
frames routinely. Treating that as the weight having been set down would end a
set mid-repetition, so the loaded state is tri-state rather than boolean:
observed, within a grace window, or genuinely absent --- plus a fourth case,
never observed at all, which is distinct from observed-and-lost.

The grace window is graded by the corroborating evidence available. A weight
declared at setup extends it, because that is an explicit statement from the
user that a weight is in hand. Inertial motion on that side extends it further.
Postures known to occlude extend it further still. The user-facing status
distinguishes the tiers: \emph{confirmed} while the detector sees it,
\emph{recovering} while the grace window runs, and an explicit out-of-view
message only once the window expires.

That last distinction mattered: a resting arm's dumbbell leaving frame used to
mask the active arm's dumbbell genuinely being gone, because the message
required both sides to be missing. The message is now keyed to whichever side is
currently working.

Counting requires the loaded state to be positively true. Only the explicit
negative used to block, so a session in which a dumbbell was never detected at
all counted every repetition. A weight declared at setup remains sufficient on
its own, which is what carries a set through the occlusions this design exists
to tolerate.

\subsection{The forearm signal: a negative result}
\label{sec:forearm}

This subsection reports a component that does not work as designed, because the
reason is more useful than the outcome.

A single 2D camera physically cannot distinguish a curl, a press, a row, and an
overhead triceps extension by elbow angle: all four present a bent elbow. The
forearm, however, travels completely differently in each. An inertial unit
strapped to the forearm should therefore resolve the ambiguity, and the pitch
axis of the enclosure was intended to measure forearm elevation against gravity,
normalized so that 0 is hanging, 0.5 is horizontal, and 1 is vertical.

The client accumulates that elevation across each repetition and evaluates three
features: the \emph{span} it traversed, the \emph{trough} it reached, and the
\emph{peak}. Span is a difference, so any constant offset from how the enclosure
is rotated on the strap cancels out of it; trough and peak are offset-sensitive
and are used only as coarse bands. A confusion matrix over the four movements'
characteristic ranges confirmed that span alone is insufficient --- a span
threshold of 0.18 for a curl also accepts a press, a row, and a triceps
extension, because all of them move a great deal --- and that adding trough and
peak, with the curl span floor raised to 0.32, makes all sixteen cells correct
while still accepting a half-range curl.

The design is sound. The input signal is not. Live hardware disproved the
elevation mapping: a hanging arm reported a pitch of 88\textdegree{} and a roll
of $-89$\textdegree, giving an elevation of 0.99 --- ``pointing straight up'' for
an arm hanging at the side. That is both wrong for the pose and sitting in Euler
gimbal lock, where pitch near $\pm90$\textdegree{} makes roll and yaw degenerate.
Gating on it rejected every curl.

Two conclusions follow. The immediate one is that the check can only advise: it
labels its message a form tip rather than claiming to have blocked anything, and
the repetition counts regardless. The more useful one is what would fix it, and
neither option is speculative. Either record the hanging-arm orientation at
calibration and measure every subsequent reading relative to it, which converts
an absolute claim into a differential one; or derive elevation from the
accelerometer's gravity vector directly, which has no gimbal-lock singularity
because it does not pass through an Euler representation at all. The second is
the better engineering answer and is the one Section~\ref{sec:futurework}
recommends.

\code{forearm\_elevation} is exported to \code{signal\_log} on every frame
precisely so the mapping can be fitted against recorded sessions once such
sessions exist. The confusion-matrix result stands as the design target.

\subsection{Grip orientation, demoted}

Curl and hammer curl differ by wrist rotation, which no camera in this setup can
see and which the inertial unit's roll axis can. That check was originally a
hard gate on the instrumented side.

It is no longer. A gate is only defensible when its evidence is reliable, and
roll shares the gimbal-lock exposure described above. Rather than delete the
signal, it was demoted: a repetition whose grip did not match the prescribed one
still counts, but is not marked sensor-verified. The signal keeps doing useful
work as a quality measure instead of acting as a blunt gate on a measurement
that cannot yet carry that weight.

This is also why the interface names which sensors are corroborating the current
exercise rather than presenting a single confidence number. With one unit on one
wrist by design, the uninstrumented arm's grip cannot be checked at all, and the
user should be able to see that asymmetry rather than infer it.

\subsection{Camera framing guidance}

The largest real-world driver of out-of-view events turned out not to be
detection logic. In a recorded session the webcam was framed tight and high, so
the arms --- and any dumbbell in them --- sat below the visible frame for long
stretches. No amount of occlusion tolerance addresses a limb that is never in
the image.

The client watches for both arm chains being simultaneously invisible, requires
that condition to persist for 4.5 seconds rather than reacting to a single bad
frame, and then suggests once that the user reposition. A 25-second cooldown
prevents it from firing repeatedly while the condition persists. The design
principle is that guidance the user cannot act on is noise, and guidance
repeated every frame is worse than none.

\subsection{The avatar}

The movement stage renders a low-polygon humanoid whose limb angles are computed
from the same normalized signals that drive counting, rather than from a
separate animation path. That is deliberate: an avatar driven independently
would be a decoration, whereas one driven by the contract is a visualization of
what the system believes is happening, and disagreements between the two are
visible rather than hidden.

Three rendering problems from a recorded session are worth documenting because
each was a reasoning error rather than a coding error.

\emph{Every exercise rendered as the same sideways reach.} The projection only
ever swung a limb away from vertical into the lateral axis, so a curl, a front
raise, and a lateral raise were geometrically identical on screen. Movements
differ by \emph{plane}, not only by magnitude, so the projection now takes a
plane bias per exercise: purely lateral for a lateral raise, toward the camera
for front raises and holds, away from it for a row's backward elbow travel and a
triceps extension's fold behind the head.

\emph{Extended arms rendered bent.} Each mode gave the forearm a constant
angular offset from the upper arm. The offsets look negligible as raw angles ---
14\textdegree{} in one case --- but the swing component diverges sharply near
vertical, where $\cos 80^{\circ} = 0.17$ against $\cos 66^{\circ} = 0.41$,
so a small angular difference rendered as an obvious kink at the elbow. Each
mode is now written so that when its driving signal reports the arm extended,
the forearm angle equals the upper-arm angle. A press is the exception, because
``extended'' there is the top of the repetition rather than the bottom, so its
invariant is asserted at lockout.

\emph{The dumbbell hung vertically like a totem pole.} The dumbbell inherited
the forearm bone's quaternion. Aligning a bone constrains where its axis points
but leaves its roll about that axis arbitrary --- invisible for a
roll-symmetric cylinder, obvious for a bar with plates on the ends. The
dumbbell now builds its own orientation, with its bar axis as the cross product
of the forearm direction and world up, which is naturally perpendicular to the
arm and level regardless of where the forearm points, matching how a gripped
dumbbell actually sits.

A fourth issue was environmental rather than geometric: the renderer was sized
without accounting for device pixel ratio, which happened to be correct at a
ratio of 1 and broke at any browser zoom step or on a high-density display. It
is now reconciled from three independent triggers --- window resize, a resize
observer on the mount, and a per-frame check --- so any desync self-corrects
within one frame.

\subsection{Threats to validity of the detector evaluation}
\label{sec:leakage}

Two properties of the merged dataset limit how far these figures generalize, and
both were identified after the training run.

An exact-duplicate audit by SHA-1 digest over all 7{,}332 images found 7{,}276
unique digests. Forty-nine groups of byte-identical images exist, of which 24
span more than one split. Twenty-four evaluation images are byte-identical to a
training image: 20 of 1{,}093 validation images (1.8\,\%) and 4 of 571 test images
(0.7\,\%).

The more consequential property concerns how the splits were formed. Filenames
retain their source prefixes, and grouping by prefix shows that 5{,}669 of 7{,}332
images (77.3\,\%) belong to source groups whose frames appear in more than one
split. The largest group alone contributes 2{,}121 images distributed as
1{,}842 train, 166 validation, and 113 test. These are consecutive frames from
continuous video, so evaluation images are frequently near-duplicates of training
images even when they are not byte-identical.

The consequence is that the reported metrics measure performance on scenes the
model has seen rather than on unseen scenes, and should be read as an upper bound
rather than as an estimate of generalization. The figures are retained because
they document the model actually deployed in the system, but any comparison
against other detectors, or any claim of generalization, requires re-partitioning
the dataset so that all frames from one source appear in exactly one split, and
retraining. This is stated as the first item of future work in
Section~\ref{sec:futurework}.

\section{Positioning Relative to Existing Work in the Lab}
\label{sec:positioning}

This section situates FitQuest against the Persuasive Computing Lab's published
work, to identify what the project adds to the group's existing capability and
where it depends on methods the group already has. It characterizes the lab's
published output; it does not claim knowledge of unpublished internal work.

\subsection{The lab's established approach}

The Persuasive Computing Lab, directed by Dr.\ Rita Orji, works at the
intersection of persuasive technology, human--computer interaction, and digital
health, with an emphasis on behaviour change, personalization, and
technology for under-served populations~\cite{orji-bio,pcl}. Physical activity
is a recurring application domain, and the group has published systematic
reviews of persuasive strategies in mobile physical-activity
interventions~\cite{review-pa} and of social exergames~\cite{review-exergames}.

Three recent systems are directly relevant:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline,itemsep=3pt]
\item[FITPULSE] An AI-driven persuasive exergame built on wearable sensing and
human activity recognition~\cite{fitpulse}. This is the closest antecedent to
FitQuest within the group, and shares two of the present supervisors as authors.
\item[AR Dancee] A mobile augmented-reality intervention promoting physical
activity through dancing, evaluated in a 15-day study with 104
participants~\cite{ardancee}.
\item[CalmMe] An adaptive system for stress detection and management that reads
heart-rate variability from an Apple Watch through HealthKit and classifies
stress with a convolutional network~\cite{calmme}. It also shares two of the
present supervisors as authors.
\end{description}

Two characteristics recur across this work. Sensing is obtained from commodity
consumer devices through their vendor APIs --- smartphones and smartwatches ---
rather than from purpose-built hardware. And evaluation is conducted with
participants, over multi-day protocols, with statistical analysis of behavioural
outcomes.

\subsection{What FitQuest adds}

FitQuest differs on the sensing side and is deliberately incomplete on the
evaluation side.

Three contributions follow from this comparison.

First, the project produces an \textbf{instrumented sensing substrate} the group
did not previously have. Commodity-device sensing is constrained by what a
vendor API exposes; a purpose-built inertial unit gives direct access to
acceleration, angular velocity, and orientation at a chosen rate and mounting
position. The tradeoff is explicit: bespoke hardware requires assembly,
enclosure design, and firmware maintenance that a commodity device does not.

Second, the \textbf{signal specification is reusable across projects}. Because
the browser client consumes only normalized values, a different application
--- a rehabilitation task, an adherence monitor, a different exergame --- can be
built against the same specification without touching the sensing code. Within a
lab that repeatedly builds physical-activity interventions, this decoupling is
the component most likely to be reused.

Third, and conversely, \textbf{the project's principal gap is precisely the
lab's established strength}. FitQuest has not been evaluated with participants,
so no claim is made about engagement, adherence, or behaviour change. The
sensible reading is complementary rather than competitive: this work supplies an
instrumented, documented sensing layer, and the group's existing evaluation
methodology is what would establish whether interventions built on it change
behaviour.

\subsection{Implications for the group}

If the sensing layer is adopted, three practical consequences follow.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Signal granularity increases.} Commodity smartwatch APIs report
aggregate activity; this pipeline reports per-side arm extension, range
utilization, bilateral symmetry, torso hinge, and stability at frame rate. That
granularity is what would allow an intervention to respond to \emph{how} a
movement was performed rather than only that activity occurred.

\item \textbf{Hardware maintenance becomes a cost.} Firmware, enclosure
revisions, battery handling, and mounting all require attention that commodity
devices absorb on the researcher's behalf. This cost is worth stating before the
approach is adopted for a study.

\item \textbf{Deployment constraints tighten.} As Section~\ref{sec:network}
records, the demonstrated configuration requires a shared subnet and internet
access. A participant study outside a controlled space would need the
self-hosted relay noted there, or the reduced offline configuration.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the fusion actually bought}

The premise was that three modalities cover each other's blind spots. It is
worth separating where that held from where it did not, because the answer is
uneven and the uneven parts are the informative ones.

It held for occlusion. The camera loses the dumbbell behind the torso on most
repetitions, and inertial motion on that side is what justifies extending the
grace window rather than declaring the weight set down. This is the clearest
case of one modality covering another's known failure, and it is the reason
sets survive realistic camera angles.

It held for confidence rather than for correctness. Inertial motion and watch
motion state raise the sensor-verified count, which is reported alongside the
raw count. That is a genuine use --- a session where most repetitions were
corroborated is different evidence from one where none were --- but it does not
change what is counted.

It did not hold for the ambiguity it was most expected to resolve. A single
camera cannot distinguish curl, press, row, and triceps extension by elbow
angle, the forearm's trajectory does distinguish them, and the design that
exploits this was validated on paper. The signal it needs was not available
because the orientation mapping was wrong (Section~\ref{sec:forearm}). What
resolved those exercises instead was a camera signal: the upper arm's angle
relative to the torso, which is visible in 2D and which the original design had
not treated as load-bearing.

That reversal is the most transferable finding here. The modality expected to
carry the discrimination did not, a second camera-derived geometric feature
did, and the reason was not sensor quality but an uncalibrated frame
relationship. A system integrating a body-worn sensor should treat
sensor-to-segment calibration as a first-class requirement rather than a
refinement, because without it the sensor's most distinctive capability is
unavailable regardless of how good the hardware is.

\subsection{Implications for design}

Four lessons generalize beyond this system.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline,itemsep=5pt]
\item[Let evidence corroborate, not veto] In a multi-sensor interactive system,
allowing every sensor to reject an event produces worse behaviour than treating
most as corroboration. A single stale input should reduce confidence, not halt
the interaction. The failure mode when this is wrong is particularly bad: the
system stops responding while every component reports itself healthy, and the
user has no way to see why.

\item[A rule is only enforceable when its evidence is observable] Rules
conditioned on state that may be untracked must check observability first.
Enforcing alternation using an arm the camera cannot see punishes correct
performance for a tracking failure --- and from the user's side that is
indistinguishable from the system being broken.

\item[Every latched state needs an expiry] Two separate defects here were
states that could be entered and never left: a limb stuck in the bent phase,
and a stale negative for a dumbbell that had reappeared. Neither was reachable
in component testing because each required a specific sequence that only a real
session produces.

\item[Report the mechanism's quality, not just its completion] Calibration can
satisfy its timer and sample-count conditions while capturing a range too
narrow to be useful. A mechanism that can complete in a degenerate state should
report that state rather than let downstream consumers assume success. This
generalizes to any adaptive component with an initialization phase.
\end{description}

\subsection{Where component testing was insufficient}

Every correctness defect described in this report was found in a session with
all hardware present, and none by the automated suite --- which nonetheless
passes 62 tests and is worth keeping, because its role is preventing the return
of defects rather than discovering them.

The pattern is specific enough to act on. The defects that escaped were
interaction defects between components that were each individually correct: a
state machine correct in isolation but never reset by the path that dismissed
its trigger; a duplicated formula updated in one copy and not the other, where
both copies were internally consistent; a rule whose evidence was assumed
present. Testing components in isolation cannot surface any of these by
construction.

What would have caught them is not more unit tests but recorded end-to-end
sessions replayed against the pipeline. The frame-level export format already
carries what such a replay needs, and the offline simulator already demonstrates
that the client can be driven from a stream rather than from hardware. The
missing piece is a corpus of recorded real sessions to replay --- which the
participant study in Section~\ref{sec:studyreqs} would produce as a by-product.

\subsection{On the scope change}

The narrowing in week six is the decision the rest of the work depended on, and
it is worth stating what made it correct rather than merely necessary.

The original proposal had three deliverables whose dependencies ran in series:
instrumented hardware, a game platform, and adherence analytics that required
both to be operational. Attempting all three would have produced three
incomplete components and no defensible claim. Narrowing to the intermediate
layer produced something completable within the time available, and --- more
importantly --- something whose value does not depend on the other two ever
being built.

The cost is equally real. The system prescribes exercise and demonstrates
interaction, but demonstrates nothing about adherence, engagement, or behaviour
change, which is the outcome the host lab's research is ultimately concerned
with. This work supplies an instrumented substrate; establishing whether
interventions built on it change behaviour requires the methodology the group
already has and this project deliberately did not attempt.

\subsection{Weeks 1--5: foundation (May 18 -- June 22)}

The opening phase established the vision pipeline. The base YOLO26 pose and

This phase also produced the assessment that led to the change of scope. With the
vision component operational, the remaining work required by the original
proposal --- custom dumbbell hardware, a complete 3D game, and longitudinal
analytics --- exceeded the time available.

\subsection{Week 6: change of scope (June 23 -- June 28)}

Scope was formally renarrowed. The abandoned keypoint-analysis path was removed in
full, \code{game\_control} was reworked from a game-input structure into a general
sensor-fusion payload, auto-calibration and normalized arm signals were added,
heart rate was mapped to \code{exertion\_level} and \code{intensity\_zone}, and
flat \code{signal\_log} records were introduced. The documentation was rewritten
around the revised deliverable.

The pivot report recorded five risks. Reviewed at the close of the internship,
four materialized in some form: the detector required substantial geometric
filtering; the inertial mounting introduced an orientation offset requiring an
excursion-based approach; the wearable path required a relay; and camera-derived
signals required validation before use. The fifth, avoiding any implication that
the game is complete, was addressed through scope definition.

\subsection{Week 7: inertial bring-up (June 29 -- July 5)}

The ESP32-S3 and BNO08x were assembled and validated. The I\textsuperscript{2}C
scanner confirmed the sensor at address \code{0x4B}, firmware was written to
publish structured telemetry, and both the USB serial and Wi-Fi UDP paths were
confirmed against the Python bridge. Five capture sessions were recorded during
this period, covering still, tilt-sweep, and motion-burst conditions, and
processed through the analysis pipeline. Those summaries recorded an effective
sample rate of approximately 15\,Hz and median pose confidence above 0.84.

The breadboard assembly was functional but insufficiently robust for movement
capture, which established the requirement for the enclosure.

\subsection{Week 8: wearable integration (July 6 -- July 12)}

The transport layer was unified so that a single launcher command listens on USB
serial and Wi-Fi concurrently, removing the need to select a transport before each
run. The BLE heart-rate bridge was added, the wearable specification was extended
to carry heart-rate contact, resting and maximum references, and optional RR
intervals, and the offline analysis was extended to summarize wearable status,

\section{Repository Consolidation}
\label{sec:consolidation}

Reproducibility being a requirement for the research output, the final week was
allocated to consolidating the repository. The review covered tracked files,
cross-file references, and content excluded by version control.

\subsection{Removals}

Superseded and unreferenced material was removed: a training launcher duplicating
logic present in the command-line interface; an unreferenced dataset preparation
script; a synthetic heart-rate generator and its sample file; a workspace
organizer; a legacy regression fixture and its route; the serial-only firmware
variant; a deprecated watch application and its sideloading test; a Blender
autosave and a superseded G-code revision; and four presentation iterations
totalling approximately 14\,MB. Tracked files decreased from 124 to 83, and
subsequently to 96 once the evidence figures described below were brought under
version control.

\subsection{Unification}

Two commands defined approximately fifty command-line options in nearly identical
blocks; these now share a single definition and differ only through an explicit
defaults table, which also documents the differences. A compatibility class whose
only output was pose liveness was folded into the keypoint module. Unused browser
code and style rules were removed.

Two defects were identified during this work. The presentation module contained a
copy of the inertial intensity function written before an angular-velocity term
was added to the runtime version; the monitor consequently under-reported
movement, reporting 0.025 where the runtime and browser reported 0.617 for the
same input. The monitor now imports the runtime function. Separately, one
command's serial port default disabled the serial transport, making an explicitly
requested serial connection inoperative. Both defects resulted from duplicated
logic updated in only one location, and neither was detectable by the test suite,
since each copy was internally consistent.

\subsection{Preservation of evidence}

Training output is excluded from version control because a single run produces
several hundred megabytes. That exclusion also covered the confusion matrix,
precision-recall curves, and per-epoch metrics for the detector, which are the
measurements this report cites and which existed on a single machine without
backup. That subset is now committed with a document recording the run that
produced it. Five capture sessions were similarly reduced from 54\,MB of video and
frames to 5.6\,KB of analysis summaries before the raw media was discarded.

The relay worker's source was committed without its deployment configuration,
which meant the service could be read but not rebuilt from the repository. A
deployment configuration was added.

\subsection{Bringing the system up from nothing}

The order matters, because each step gives a verifiable result before the next
one can hide a failure inside it.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Software only.} Create the environment, install requirements, run
the test suite. Sixty-two tests should pass in a few seconds with no hardware
attached. If they do not, nothing downstream is worth attempting.

\item \textbf{Browser client without hardware.} Run the offline simulator and
open the printed URL. This exercises the gateway, the client, the avatar, and
the full exercise flow against a synthetic stream. A failure here is in software,
not in wiring.

\item \textbf{Camera alone.} Start the runtime with the inertial transport
disabled. Confirm the pose skeleton tracks and dumbbells are detected.

\item \textbf{Inertial over USB.} Flash the scanner sketch first and confirm
the sensor answers at \code{0x4B}. Only then flash telemetry firmware and read
it with the standalone check command. Close any serial monitor first --- it
holds the port, and the resulting failure looks like a hardware fault.

\item \textbf{Inertial over Wi-Fi.} Both devices on the same subnet. This is
where most environment-specific trouble appears; Section~\ref{sec:network}
explains why a phone hotspot rather than the campus network became the standard
configuration.

\item \textbf{Watch.} Sideload the Connect IQ application and confirm the
relay is receiving. The watch screen reports its own transmission state, which
is faster to read than the laptop side.

\item \textbf{Everything together.} Only now is a full session meaningful.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Known pitfalls}

Each of these cost time during the internship and is not obvious from the code.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline,itemsep=4pt]
\item[A serial monitor holds the port] and the resulting failure is
indistinguishable from a disconnected board.

\item[Two wearable writers overwrite each other] A BLE missing-device state
will overwrite a valid Connect IQ sample in the shared file. The BLE path is
disabled by default for this reason; enable it only when Connect IQ is not
running.

\item[The campus network will not carry the inertial telemetry] It uses
enterprise authentication the firmware does not implement, and does not reliably
permit UDP broadcast between clients.

\item[A Connect IQ timer started from the application lifecycle crashes]
on Venu 3 firmware 17.05. It must be started from the view lifecycle. This is
recorded because the crash log names a line number rather than a cause.

\item[Wireless operation needs a power source] The board draws over USB; see
Section~\ref{sec:power}.

\item[The detector proposes dumbbells liberally] Raw detections are not
trustworthy on their own and are gated geometrically. Anyone tempted to consume
\code{object\_detection} directly should read Section~\ref{sec:results} first.
\end{description}

\subsection{Removing the external dependency}
\label{sec:selfhost}

The only component requiring infrastructure outside the lab is the wearable
relay, and it exists for a specific reason: Connect IQ requires HTTPS, and a
phone acting as both hotspot gateway and Garmin bridge will not route the
watch's request back to a laptop on that hotspot.

Any HTTPS endpoint reachable from the phone satisfies both conditions. A
lab-hosted service with a valid certificate would replace the current relay
without changes to the middleware, since the runtime reads the wearable sample
from a local file and is indifferent to which bridge produced it. The changes
required are the endpoint constant in the watch application, a rebuild and
re-sideload, and a poller pointed at the new address. If the lab prefers its own
database rather than the current single-row store, note that the relay retains
only the most recent sample by design --- adding history is a schema decision,
not an architectural one.

\section{Requirements for a Participant Study}
\label{sec:studyreqs}

The most valuable next step is an evaluation with independent participants, and
the report has said so repeatedly. This section states concretely what such a
study would need, because several of the requirements are instrumentation gaps
in the current system rather than protocol decisions.

\subsection{What the system cannot currently measure}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Ground truth for repetitions.} The system counts, but nothing
records what actually happened. A study needs synchronized video annotated by a
human, or a second observer counting independently, so that counting accuracy
can be computed rather than asserted. Frame-level export already carries a
timestamp per record, so aligning an external annotation is a matter of
protocol rather than of new code. The recording corpus described in
Section~\ref{sec:recordings} is the obvious starting point: annotating the four
July validation recordings would yield a first accuracy figure without
collecting any new data, though those recordings capture the developer rather
than independent participants and so bound what the resulting figure can
support.

\item \textbf{End-to-end latency.} The time from physical movement to visible
response has never been measured. For an interactive system this is a primary
quality attribute and a plausible confound: a participant reacting to a laggy
avatar behaves differently from one reacting to a responsive one.

\item \textbf{Session duration under battery.} No power budget exists
(Section~\ref{sec:power}). A protocol cannot specify session length responsibly
without knowing when the inertial unit stops transmitting.

\item \textbf{Per-participant calibration records.} Calibration bounds are
computed and used, but a study testing cross-user comparability --- the claim in
RQ2 --- must retain each participant's bounds for analysis. They appear in the
payload; they are not currently persisted separately.

\item \textbf{A session identifier.} Records are per-frame and per-session in
practice but carry no explicit participant or session key, which makes
multi-participant analysis awkward and pseudonymization impossible.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Protocol elements}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline,itemsep=4pt]
\item[Inclusion criteria] Prior resistance-training experience is a plausible
covariate: the calibration window assumes the participant moves through a
representative range, which an inexperienced participant may not do.

\item[Fitting procedure] The enclosure's rotation on the strap sets an offset
that the current system does not calibrate out (Section~\ref{sec:forearm}). Until
that is fixed, the fitting procedure must be identical across participants and
recorded, or the inertial data is not comparable between them.

\item[Comparison conditions] The claim being tested determines these. For
counting accuracy, camera-only against full fusion isolates what the additional
sensors contribute. For usability, a comparison against an unassisted session,
or against an existing application, is more informative than a single-condition
trial.

\item[Instruments] A usability scale, and a workload instrument if cognitive
load is of interest. Neither is currently administered.

\item[Adverse events] The system prescribes exercise. A protocol needs a stated
procedure for a participant reporting discomfort, and the enclosure has not been
assessed for pressure or interference under heavier loads.
\end{description}

\subsection{Ethics and data handling}

Section~\ref{sec:privacy} lists the gaps. For a participant study specifically,
the binding ones are institutional ethics approval, informed consent that
covers camera recording and physiological measurement explicitly, a decision
recorded in advance about whether identifiable imagery is retained at all, and
authentication on the relay endpoints before any participant data passes through
them.

The strongest position is to record no identifiable imagery. The pipeline
processes frames in memory and writes them only when capture is explicitly
requested, so a study can be run with imagery never persisted --- which removes
the largest category of risk at no cost to the signals under study.

\section{Privacy and Security}
\label{sec:privacy}

The system handles camera imagery and heart-rate data, both of which are
identifiable or sensitive. The current configuration is an experimental one and
is documented here as such rather than presented as deployment-ready.

\subsection{Current handling}

Camera frames are processed in memory and are not written to disk during a normal
session. The capture command writes frames and video only when explicitly
invoked. Wearable samples are written to a local JSON file and, when the relay is
in use, to a Cloudflare D1 record holding the most recent sample. Frame-level
JSONL export contains derived signals and heart rate, not imagery.

\subsection{Identified gaps}

\begin{itemize}
\item The relay's \code{POST /garmin} endpoint is unauthenticated unless
      \code{FITQUEST\_SHARED\_TOKEN} is configured, and \code{GET /latest} has no
      authentication in either configuration.
\item The local Connect IQ receiver binds to all interfaces without
      authentication.
\item The inertial discovery token ships as a placeholder value.
\item No retention or deletion policy is defined for the relay record or for
      local capture sessions.
\item The wearable schema accepts location fields that the system does not use.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Requirements before participant use}

Any evaluation involving participants would require, at minimum: authentication
on both relay endpoints; removal of unused sensitive fields from the schema,
location in particular; a stated retention and deletion policy; pseudonymized
session identifiers; informed consent covering camera recording and physiological
measurement; institutional ethics approval; and a decision, recorded in advance,
about whether identifiable imagery is retained at all. None of these are in place,
which is one reason the system has not been evaluated with participants.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline,itemsep=4pt]
\item[Single-camera depth] Body posture is estimated from one 2D camera. Depth is
approximated from apparent scale rather than measured.

\item[Detector evaluation] The reported metrics were obtained on a randomly
partitioned dataset in which 77.3\,\% of images belong to source groups spanning
more than one split (Section~\ref{sec:leakage}). They characterize the deployed
model but do not establish generalization to unseen scenes.

\item[Unmeasured filter contribution] The confidence, area, and proximity filters
that gate raw detections were derived from observed behaviour rather than from
measurement. Their individual contribution has not been isolated.

\item[Single-side inertial verification] With one glove, grip-rotation
differences on the uninstrumented arm cannot be verified by any current sensor and
continue to be counted from the camera's elbow-bend signal alone.

\item[Mounting offset] Enclosure rotation on the strap determines an absolute
pitch offset. Absolute forearm elevation is therefore an approximate value, and
validation relies on excursion instead.

\item[Wearable latency and validity] Heart rate is a context and intensity signal
rather than a control or safety signal. Samples arrive at a 3\,s interval, and
wrist-based optical heart rate has not been validated against a reference
instrument under the forearm movement this application involves.

\item[Network dependency] The demonstrated configuration requires a shared local
subnet for the wireless inertial path and internet access for the wearable relay
(Section~\ref{sec:network}). Only the reduced configuration --- inertial unit over
USB and watch over BLE --- operates with no network at all.

\item[Carried power supply] The inertial unit draws power over USB, so wireless
operation requires a power bank carried by the user and cabled to the forearm
(Section~\ref{sec:power}). The enclosure has no provision for a supply, and no
power budget or session-duration figure has been measured.

\item[Device-frame orientation] Inertial orientation is reported in the device
frame and has not been transformed into an anatomical frame. Pitch and roll are
therefore not forearm joint angles, and yaw is relative because the firmware uses
a magnetometer-free fusion mode.

\item[No participant evaluation] The system has been validated technically and
exercised in full sessions by the author. It has not been evaluated with
independent participants, and no claim is made regarding usability, engagement, or
adherence.

\item[Calibration assumes cooperation] The calibration window assumes the user
moves through a representative range. Quality reporting identifies when this does
not occur but cannot correct the resulting session.

\item[Proof-of-concept application] The browser client demonstrates the sensing
path. It is not a fitness product.
\end{description}

\section{Improvement Opportunities and Future Work}
\label{sec:futurework}

The prototype reached a working state within the internship, and every subsystem
has an identifiable path to greater accuracy or robustness. This section sets
those paths out by subsystem, with the reasoning behind each, so that the lab can
decide which are worth the effort for its own research objectives. The
recommendations are offered for consideration; the priorities depend on which
research questions the group intends to pursue.

\subsection{Vision: deeper training of pose, object detection, and tracking}

The vision layer currently uses a stock body-pose model and a detector trained on
general annotated sources. It works, but it is the subsystem with the most
accessible improvement, because improvement here requires data collection rather
than new hardware.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Source-disjoint re-partition and retraining.} The dataset should be
regrouped so that all frames originating from one video or capture session appear
in exactly one split, then retrained and re-evaluated. Until this is done, the
metrics in Section~\ref{sec:results} cannot support a claim of generalization.
Reporting across several seeds would additionally give a variance estimate.

\item \textbf{Domain-specific detector data.} Images captured in the deployment
setting --- the lab's specific weights, camera placement, lighting, and occlusion
conditions --- together with a negative set containing watches, phones, bottles,
and bare hands, would improve coverage and provide a defined denominator for
measuring false positives.

\item \textbf{Fine-tuning the pose model.} The pose model is used as published.
Fine-tuning on annotated frames of dumbbell exercises specifically, where arms
cross the torso and the weight occludes the wrist, would address the failure
mode that currently requires temporal smoothing to mask.

\item \textbf{Temporal pose models.} The current pipeline estimates pose
independently per frame and smooths afterwards. A model that consumes a frame
sequence directly would exploit the fact that human movement is continuous, and
would likely reduce the joint-level jitter that motivated the smoothing layer.

\item \textbf{Learned activity recognition.} The system identifies exercises
through geometric rules over calibrated signals. A classifier trained on the
recorded \code{signal\_log} stream could recognize exercises directly and would
extend more easily to movements not anticipated by the rules. The geometric
signals would remain useful as interpretable features for such a model.

\item \textbf{Full-body and multi-person tracking.} The pipeline selects the
single most confident person and analyses the upper body. Extending to lower-body
joints would support squats, lunges, and gait; handling multiple people would
support paired or group sessions. Both are substantial changes to the association
logic, not parameter adjustments.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Inertial subsystem: accuracy and methodology}

The inertial unit is functional but was configured for reliable operation rather
than for measurement accuracy. Several changes would improve it, in roughly
increasing order of effort.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Use the magnetometer for absolute heading.} The firmware requests
the game rotation vector, which fuses accelerometer and gyroscope but excludes
the magnetometer. Yaw is therefore relative and drifts over a session. Requesting
the full rotation vector would give an absolute heading reference, at the cost of
sensitivity to the magnetic disturbance that gym equipment and the enclosure's
own electronics produce. Whether this trade is favourable is an empirical
question worth testing rather than assuming.

\item \textbf{Raise the sample rate.} Telemetry is published every 66\,ms
($\approx$15\,Hz). The sensor supports considerably higher rates. A repetition
lasting one to two seconds is described by only 15--30 samples at present, which
is adequate for detecting that movement occurred but marginal for characterizing
its velocity profile or identifying the sticking point within a lift.

\item \textbf{Sensor-to-segment calibration.} Orientation is currently reported
in the device frame, and the enclosure's rotation on the strap introduces an
unknown constant offset --- which is why validation uses excursion rather than
absolute angle. A functional calibration procedure, in which the user holds two
or three known poses so the rotation between device frame and anatomical frame
can be computed, would convert device-frame orientation into forearm angles that
are comparable across sessions and users. This is the single change that would
most improve the inertial data's analytical value.

\item \textbf{Explicit orientation filtering.} The current motion intensity is a
heuristic combination of acceleration, angular velocity, and orientation deltas.
A complementary, Madgwick, Mahony, or Kalman filter over the raw signals would
produce a principled orientation estimate with characterized noise behaviour,
replacing thresholds chosen by observation.

\item \textbf{Multiple inertial units per limb.} A single unit measures the
segment it is attached to. Units on the upper arm and forearm together yield the
elbow angle directly, without the camera and without the projection ambiguity a
single 2D view introduces. This would also remove the single-side verification
limitation noted in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\item \textbf{Reference-instrument validation.} None of the above can be
evaluated without a ground truth. Comparison against an optical motion-capture
system, a goniometer, or a commercial inertial suite, over a set of prescribed
movements, would establish the accuracy of the orientation estimate rather than
leaving it as a design assumption.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Wearable subsystem: accuracy and alternative access paths}

The Garmin Venu 3 currently contributes heart rate and coarse wrist motion at a
3\,s interval. Its measurement value is limited less by the device than by the
access path chosen.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Reduce the transmission interval.} The 3\,s interval is a value set
in the watch application, chosen conservatively for battery and network
behaviour. Characterizing the actual cost of a shorter interval would establish
whether wrist motion can contribute at a rate useful for repetition-level
corroboration rather than only session-level context.

\item \textbf{Transmit the fields the schema already accepts.} The Python and
relay schemas accept RR intervals and gyroscope data that the watch application
does not currently produce. RR intervals in particular would allow
heart-rate variability to be derived, which is a substantially better exertion
indicator than instantaneous heart rate.

\item \textbf{Individualized exertion bounds.} Exertion is normalized against
default resting and maximum heart rates when the payload does not supply
personalized values. Using each participant's measured resting rate, and an
age-predicted or tested maximum, would make the exertion signal comparable
between individuals rather than only within one session.

\item \textbf{Post-session data at higher fidelity.} Connect IQ provides live
data under real-time constraints. The Garmin Health API or exported activity
files provide higher-resolution records after a session. For analysis that does
not need to be live --- which includes most research analysis --- this is the
better source, and it would complement rather than replace the live path.

\item \textbf{Reference-instrument validation.} Wrist-based optical heart rate is
known to degrade during movement involving the forearm, which is exactly the
condition here. Comparison against a chest-strap electrocardiographic monitor
during dumbbell exercise would quantify that degradation for this specific use,
and would determine whether the heart-rate signal is fit for the exertion
interpretation placed on it.

\item \textbf{Self-hosted relay.} Replacing the external relay with a local
HTTPS endpoint would satisfy the Connect IQ transport requirement while removing
the internet dependency described in Section~\ref{sec:network}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Physical enclosure, attachment, and placement}

The enclosure reached a second printed revision and holds the electronics
securely. Its remaining limitations concern how and where it attaches, which
affects measurement quality directly rather than only comfort.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Repeatable mounting orientation.} The enclosure can currently be
strapped on at any rotation, and that rotation sets the pitch offset that forces
validation to rely on excursion. A keyed or indexed interface --- a feature that
permits only one mounting orientation --- would make the offset constant and
known across sessions, and would make the sensor-to-segment calibration described
above far simpler to apply.

\item \textbf{Attachment mechanism.} The current arrangement is a strap. The
mechanism determines how much the enclosure moves relative to the limb during
rapid motion, and that relative movement appears in the data as acceleration the
arm did not experience. A wider strap, a compliant backing that conforms to the
limb, or a rigid cradle with two anchor points would each reduce it. The
mechanism should also be quick to fit, since a study requires it to be applied
consistently to many participants.

\item \textbf{Placement on the limb.} Placement is a trade-off that has not been
tested. A distal position near the wrist experiences the largest angular
excursion, giving the strongest signal, but sits where soft tissue moves most and
where the watch is also worn. A proximal position near the elbow is more stable
but registers less of the movement. A mid-forearm position is a compromise
adopted by default rather than by measurement. Recording the same prescribed
movements at each position and comparing the resulting signals would settle this
empirically.

\item \textbf{Glove against armband.} Project documentation refers to both a
glove and a forearm armband, reflecting an early intention and the current
implementation respectively. A glove-mounted sensor is closer to the load and
captures grip rotation more directly, which matters for distinguishing a curl
from a hammer curl; an armband is easier to fit across hand sizes and does not
interfere with grip. The choice should be made deliberately and the terminology
then unified.

\item \textbf{Onboard power.} This is the change that would most improve the
practical usability of the wireless path. The enclosure houses the board but not
a supply, so untethered operation currently depends on a USB power bank carried
separately with a cable to the forearm (Section~\ref{sec:power}). Two options
are worth evaluating, and they trade against each other:

\begin{itemize}
\item A compartment sized for a \emph{small} power bank, mounted on the same
strap. This keeps a standard, replaceable, rechargeable part and needs no
charging circuit in the design, at the cost of bulk on the arm. The compartment
should be dimensioned against a specific model rather than generically, since
capacity, thickness, and connector position vary widely.
\item An integrated battery holder --- AA/AAA cells with a boost converter, or a
LiPo cell with a charging and protection circuit. This is the smaller and better
balanced result, and the only one that removes the external cable entirely, but
it adds electronics to design, a charging path to provide, and lithium handling
and safety considerations that a commercial power bank has already solved.
\end{itemize}

Either option requires strain relief at the connector, since the cable is
currently both a mechanical disturbance in the signal and a session-interruption
risk if it pulls loose. Whichever is chosen, session duration should be measured
rather than assumed: the board transmits continuously over Wi-Fi, which is the
dominant draw, and no power budget has been established.

\item \textbf{Comfort and safety under load.} The enclosure has not been
evaluated for pressure points, heat, or interference with grip during heavier
lifting. Any participant study would need this assessed before use.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Evaluation and analysis}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Participant evaluation.} The calibration mechanism is intended to
provide cross-user transferability, and this property is currently supported by
design rather than by measurement. An evaluation with independent participants,
with repetition counts scored against manual annotation, would establish counting
accuracy and calibration transferability together. This is the highest-value next
step, and it is the step for which the lab's existing methodology is already
suited.

\item \textbf{Filter ablation.} The confidence, area-ratio, body-association, and
temporal-tracking filters should be evaluated in isolation and in combination, so
that the contribution of each is measured rather than assumed.

\item \textbf{End-to-end latency measurement.} The time from physical movement to
visible response in the browser has not been measured. For an interactive system
this is a primary quality attribute.

\item \textbf{Tempo as a published signal.} Frame and sample timing are already
recorded; publishing tempo would complete the repetition, range, and tempo triad
described in the original proposal.

\item \textbf{Longitudinal analysis.} The frame-level export format was designed
for cross-session analysis and has not yet been applied over time.

\item \textbf{Rehabilitation applications.} The signal set --- range utilization,
bilateral symmetry, stability, exertion --- corresponds to measures used in
rehabilitation assessment. Pursuing this would require the sensor-to-segment
calibration and reference validation described above, since rehabilitation
measures must be defensible in anatomical terms.
\end{enumerate}

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

The internship delivered a sensor-fusion system that converts camera, inertial,
and physiological measurements into a documented, per-user calibrated signal
specification, together with a browser application that consumes that
specification to prescribe exercises, validate repetitions against multi-sensor
evidence, and provide feedback during a session.

The change of scope in week six was the decision that made completion possible.
The original proposal contained three substantial deliverables with sequential
dependencies; pursuing all three would have produced three incomplete components.
Identifying the intermediate layer as the contribution, fixing the vision scope,
and reducing the application to a proof of concept produced a plan that could be
completed and validated within the available time.

Three observations extend beyond the immediate project. Detector metrics reported
on a randomly partitioned dataset of video frames measure recall of scenes the
model has already seen: 77.3\,\% of the images used here belong to source groups
spanning more than one split, which places an upper bound on what the reported
mAP can support. In a multi-sensor system, permitting every sensor to reject an
event produces less robust behaviour than treating most sensors as corroboration,
since a single stale input should reduce confidence rather than halt the system.
Finally, the correctness defects identified in this work were found in integrated
sessions rather than in component testing, which indicates that automated testing,
while necessary to prevent regression, does not replace full-system trials.
