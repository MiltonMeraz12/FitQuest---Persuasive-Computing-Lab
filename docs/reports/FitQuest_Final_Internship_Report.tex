\section{Executive Summary}

This report documents a twelve-week Mitacs Globalink Research Internship at the
Persuasive Computing Lab, Dalhousie University. The internship began under the
title \emph{IronQuest 3D --- Smart Dumbbell Fitness Gaming Platform} and was
deliberately renarrowed on June 23, 2026 into \emph{FitQuest}, a sensor-fusion
engine that converts camera, inertial, and physiological measurements into a
single normalized signal contract, with a small browser application built on top
of that contract to prove the complete path from physical movement to
interaction.

The core argument of the work is that the reusable research contribution is not
the game. It is the middleware: a documented, per-user calibrated,
hardware-asymmetric signal contract that any downstream application --- a game,
a rehabilitation tool, an adherence monitor --- can consume without knowing
anything about YOLO, I\textsuperscript{2}C, or Bluetooth Low Energy.

\section{Introduction and Internship Context}

\subsection{The lab and the mandate}

The Persuasive Computing Lab at Dalhousie University studies systems that
influence health and well-being behaviour. The internship was framed around
building an application or system for monitoring health and well-being, with the
specific proposal being IronQuest 3D: a smart-dumbbell fitness gaming platform in
which sensor-enabled dumbbells feed a browser-based 3D game, and repetition,
tempo, and range-of-motion signals support feedback, goal setting, analytics, and
exercise adherence.

Two constraints shaped everything that followed. First, the internship had a hard
twelve-week ceiling from a May 18, 2026 start. Second, the expected output was a
research paper due August 7, 2026 --- roughly eleven weeks in. A fitness gaming
platform is not a research contribution on that timeline; a validated sensing
method can be.

\subsection{What this document is}

This is the final report of the internship. It records what was built, in what
order, why each significant engineering decision was made, what was measured, and
what remains unresolved. It is written to serve two audiences at once: the
supervisory team, who need the internship narrative and an honest accounting of
progress; and my own future self assembling the research paper, who needs the
technical sections to be precise enough to lift directly.

Sections~\ref{sec:architecture} through~\ref{sec:decisions} and
Section~\ref{sec:results} are written as paper-ready material.
Sections~\ref{sec:chronology} and~\ref{sec:consolidation} are
internship-specific.

\subsection{Terminology}

\emph{IronQuest 3D} refers to the original proposal and remains the repository
name. \emph{FitQuest} refers to the delivered system and is the name used in the
browser client, the watch application, and the presentation material. The two
names describe the same project at different stages of scope.

\section{Scope Evolution: From IronQuest 3D to FitQuest}
\label{sec:scope}

\subsection{The problem with the original scope}

By the third week it was clear that the original scope contained a hidden
dependency chain that could not be completed and validated within the deadline:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Instrumented dumbbells require designing, building, and calibrating custom
      hardware for each weight.
\item A 3D game platform requires game design, asset production, and interaction
      tuning.
\item Adherence analytics requires longitudinal data from real users, which
      requires the first two to already work.
\end{enumerate}

Attempting all three would produce three unfinished halves. Worse, none of the
three is where the research novelty lies. The novel and defensible claim is the
middle layer: that heterogeneous, asymmetrically mounted sensors can be fused
into a single per-user calibrated contract that is stable enough for a real-time
interactive application.

\subsection{The June 23 pivot}

The project was formally renarrowed on June 23, 2026. The pivot report recorded
five decisions:

\begin{itemize}
\item The final game is deferred to a small proof-of-concept slice.
\item The vision system is frozen at body pose plus dumbbell/weight detection.
      No additional vision model enters scope.
\item The abandoned extra keypoint-analysis runtime path is removed entirely ---
      code, CLI commands, tests, tools, model runs, and documentation.
\item \code{game\_control} is reworked from a game-input structure into a
      general sensor-fusion payload.
\item Per-user auto-calibration becomes a first-class requirement rather than a
      refinement.
\end{itemize}

The last decision is the one that turned the project into research. Without
calibration, every normalized signal encodes the body proportions and range of
motion of whoever happened to develop it. With calibration, the same physical
effort maps to the same normalized value across different users, which is the
property that makes the contract reusable.

\subsection{Explicit non-goals}

Declaring non-goals in writing prevented scope creep for the remaining seven
weeks. The system deliberately does not attempt: complex game development,
multiplayer, production platform features, rule-based gym repetition counting
(that is, counting from hard-coded joint-angle thresholds without calibration),
or any vision model beyond the two named above.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{sec:architecture}

\subsection{Design principle: the payload is the contract}

The architecture is organized around one rule: \textbf{clients consume
\code{game\_control}, never raw sensor output}. The browser never sees a YOLO
bounding box, a quaternion, or a Bluetooth characteristic. It sees normalized
scalars, booleans, and labels.

This rule is what makes the work reusable. A rehabilitation application, an
adherence monitor, or a different game can be written against the same contract
without touching the sensing code. It also makes the system testable: 62
automated tests exercise the contract directly, with no camera or hardware
attached.

\subsection{Frame-level data flow}

Every camera frame passes through the following pipeline:

\begin{lstlisting}
Camera frame
  -> YOLO26 body pose (17 COCO keypoints)
  -> temporal pose smoothing (blend + occlusion hold)
  -> dumbbell/weight detector (strided, with temporal tracking)
  -> body/object side association (wrist and forearm proximity)
  -> middleware pose-stream state
  -> auto-calibrated motion signal analysis
  -> Garmin wearable context merge
  -> ESP32/IMU telemetry merge
  -> sensor-fusion game_control payload
  -> flat signal_log record
  -> OpenCV monitor / JSONL file / browser gateway
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Why the standard library for the gateway}

The browser gateway is built on Python's \code{http.server} rather than Flask,
FastAPI, or a WebSocket framework. The reasoning: the demonstration must run on a
lab laptop with no internet access and no additional service. Server-Sent Events
over plain HTTP are sufficient for a one-way payload stream at camera frame rate,
and MJPEG over \code{multipart/x-mixed-replace} is sufficient for the preview.
Adding a web framework would have introduced a dependency, a second process, and
a failure mode, in exchange for capabilities the demonstration does not use.

The preview stream is deliberately downscaled to 640\,px at JPEG quality 72. The
browser renders it inside a card roughly 290\,px wide, so encoding at full camera
resolution was approximately three times oversampled: it cost encode time on the
publishing thread and decode time in the browser, which appeared as the camera
view visibly lagging the user's real movement. Detection still runs on the
full-resolution frame, so accuracy is untouched.

\section{Sensing Subsystems}
\label{sec:sensing}

\subsection{Computer vision}

Two YOLO26 models run per frame. Body pose produces 17 COCO keypoints; a
separately trained detector produces \code{dumbbell}, \code{weight}, and
\code{other} boxes.

Three mechanisms stabilize the vision layer against the failure modes observed in
real sessions:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline,itemsep=3pt]
\item[Pose smoothing] YOLO estimates each frame independently, so lighting
changes, motion blur, or partial occlusion can make a wrist or elbow jump for a
single frame. Visible joints are blended with their recent position, and a joint
that disappears is held briefly before it is dropped from analysis.
\item[Strided object inference] The dumbbell detector runs every $N$ frames
(default 2) and cached boxes are reused between runs. Object position changes far
more slowly than the pose does, so this halves the inference cost with no
observable loss.
\item[Temporal object tracking] A detected box is held for a configurable number
of missed frames and smoothed across frames. This exists because a dumbbell
passing in front of the torso is regularly lost for two or three frames, and
without the hold the system would report the weight as dropped mid-repetition.
\end{description}

Accepted boxes must additionally pass per-class confidence floors, frame-area
ratio bounds, and a wrist-or-forearm proximity requirement before they are
allowed to mean ``loaded''. Section~\ref{sec:results} explains why this filtering
is not optional.

The firmware publishes each sample over serial \emph{and} UDP from the same
build. This was a deliberate simplification: an earlier serial-only sketch was
maintained alongside the wireless one, which meant two firmware files could drift
apart. Because the wireless sketch is a strict superset, the serial-only variant
was removed during final consolidation.

Two robustness details in the firmware are worth recording. The board calls
\code{WiFi.disconnect(true)} before each reconnect attempt, which avoids a known
ESP32 Wi-Fi library failure pattern after a dropped connection. And the laptop
broadcasts a discovery packet so the board can relearn the laptop's address after
a hotspot or network change --- necessary because the demonstration environment
frequently switched between a phone hotspot and the campus network. That
discovery packet carries a shared token, because without one any device on the
same hotspot could redirect the telemetry stream by sending its own discovery
packet.

\subsection{Wearable context: Garmin Venu 3}

The watch is treated as a \emph{context} source, never as the primary motion
sensor. Three access paths were implemented, in decreasing order of richness:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Connect IQ application.} A custom watch application posts heart
rate, optional RR intervals, accelerometer, gyroscope, and location fields.
\item \textbf{Cloudflare Worker relay.} A serverless endpoint backed by a D1
database receives the watch payload and serves the latest sample.
\item \textbf{Bluetooth Low Energy fallback.} Heart rate only, from the watch's
standard broadcast mode.
\end{enumerate}

The Worker exists because of a specific network topology problem. When the laptop
is connected to the phone's hotspot, the phone is simultaneously the hotspot
gateway and the Garmin Connect bridge, and it will not route the watch's request
back into a hotspot client. Connect IQ additionally requires HTTPS. A permanent
HTTPS relay solves both at once, and replaced an earlier approach based on
ephemeral tunnels whose URL changed on every restart --- which required
rebuilding and re-sideloading the watch application each time.

The BLE fallback is disabled by default. Running both sources against the same
wearable JSON file allowed a BLE missing-device state to overwrite a perfectly
valid Connect IQ sample.

\subsection{Physical enclosure}

The inertial hardware began as a breadboard assembly with jumper wires, which is
adequate for a desk test and unusable for movement capture. A protective case was
modelled in Blender and printed in two revisions. The repository carries the
Blender source, the exported STL, and sliced G-code for the printer profile used.

The case constrains one thing that matters analytically: the IMU's orientation
relative to the forearm. Because the rotation at which the case sits on the strap
sets an absolute pitch offset, the browser's per-repetition validation is
deliberately keyed to the \emph{excursion} of forearm elevation rather than its
absolute band, so that a constant mounting offset cancels out.

\section{The Sensor-Fusion Contract}

\subsection{Per-user auto-calibration}

At session start the system runs a calibration window (7\,s by default) during
which the user moves through a comfortable range. Per-side minimum and maximum
bounds are recorded for each feature, and subsequent measurements are normalized
against those bounds. The same physical effort therefore maps to comparable
normalized values across users of different height, limb length, and mobility ---
without retraining a model or editing source for each user.

Calibration reports a quality assessment of \code{good}, \code{limited}, or
\code{insufficient}. This exists because of a real failure mode: the timer and
sample-count gates can both pass while the user has barely moved, which produces
a near-degenerate span and therefore a saturation-prone signal for the rest of the
session. The browser's calibration control watches the live quality field rather
than closing on a fixed timer.

\subsection{Disambiguating exercises that share a signal}

A plain curl and an overhead triceps extension both bend the elbow through a
similar range. Elbow angle alone cannot separate them; only the position of the
upper arm relative to the torso can. Two signal families were added specifically
for this:

\begin{itemize}
\item \code{upper\_arm\_angle} --- from the hip--shoulder--elbow angle.
      $0^\circ$ is the arm at the side (curl); $180^\circ$ is overhead (press,
      triceps extension).
\item \code{torso\_hinge} --- from the angle between vertical and the line
      joining the hip midpoint to the shoulder midpoint. Near zero when upright,
      increasing with a forward hinge (bent-over row).
\end{itemize}

Both are published in two forms: a calibrated $[0,1]$ signal and a raw degree
measurement. \textbf{The browser's repetition gates read the raw degrees, not the
calibrated signal.} Whether an arm is near the torso or overhead is a fixed
anatomical fact and should not require per-user calibration; furthermore, an
exercise that deliberately keeps the upper arm still --- a curl --- leaves the
calibrated span near-degenerate early in a session, which made the calibrated
version unstable enough to false-trigger. The calibrated forms remain in
\code{signal\_log} for cross-user comparison in the research analysis; they
simply do not gate a repetition.

\subsection{Asymmetric hardware}

The system assumes, by default, that the inertial glove is on the right hand and
the watch on the left. Both sides are command-line configurable without code
changes. This asymmetry is not an accident of available hardware --- it is the
realistic deployment case, and handling it explicitly is part of the
contribution. It also produces an honest limitation: with one glove on one wrist,
a grip-rotation difference such as curl versus hammer curl can only be
hard-verified on the instrumented side.

\subsection{Repetition validation: hard gates and soft corroboration}

The validation design is the browser's most important technical decision, and it
came directly from real sessions rather than from theory. It separates two
categories of evidence:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline,itemsep=3pt]
\item[Hard gates] Conditions that must hold for a repetition to count at all.
These are postural preconditions read from raw degree measurements: is the upper
arm actually overhead for a press, is the torso actually hinged for a row. A hard
gate rejects a movement that is anatomically not the prescribed exercise.
\item[Soft corroboration] Additional sensor evidence that \emph{strengthens}
confidence but can never block a repetition. Glove stability, watch motion state,
forearm excursion range, and grip orientation all fall here.
\end{description}

The reason for this asymmetry is a failure observed directly in a real session:
when every sensor could veto a repetition, a single delayed or briefly stale
sensor silently froze counting, and the user had no way to tell why. Under the
current design no single sensor can permanently block a valid repetition; a
missing sensor only reduces the corroboration available.

A second correction came from the same source. An early adaptive-threshold
implementation tracked each side's lifetime minimum and maximum for the current
exercise, only ever widening. A single unusually wide or shallow early repetition
therefore skewed the threshold permanently. The current implementation uses a
6-second rolling window, so thresholds track recent range and forget an early
outlier.

\subsection{Occlusion tolerance}

A dumbbell that briefly disappears behind the torso must not invalidate the load.
The client applies a graded grace period whose length depends on the available
corroborating evidence: a declared dumbbell weight extends it, glove motion
extends it further, and postures known to be occlusion-prone extend it more
still. This is the browser-side counterpart to the temporal object tracking in
the vision layer, and the two together are what made mid-set counting survive
realistic camera angles.

Per-class behaviour from the normalized confusion matrix is more informative than
the headline mAP.

The final row is the important one. The detector finds real dumbbells well, but it
is \emph{liberal}: it proposes dumbbells in empty regions far more often than it
misses real ones. Reporting mAP@50 alone would materially overstate what the
system does in a live session.

This is precisely why the runtime does not trust raw boxes. Before any box is
allowed to mean ``the hand is loaded'', it must pass per-class confidence floors,
frame-area ratio bounds, and a wrist-or-forearm proximity requirement. The
filtered behaviour, not the raw metric, is what should be quoted when describing
system performance --- and the gap between the two is itself a finding worth
reporting: a detector that is adequate by aggregate metrics can still require
substantial geometric gating to be usable as an interaction signal.

\subsection{Weeks 1--5: foundation (May 18 -- June 22)}

The opening phase established the vision pipeline. The base YOLO26 pose and
detection weights were obtained, the merged dumbbell dataset was assembled from
two annotated sources, and the object detector was trained through its 80-epoch
run. The repository layout, capture tooling, and offline analysis pipeline were
established in this period.

This phase also produced the realization that drove the pivot. With the vision
half working, the remaining path to the originally proposed platform --- custom
dumbbell hardware, a full 3D game, and longitudinal analytics --- was visibly
longer than the time remaining.

\subsection{Week 6: the pivot (June 23 -- June 28)}

Scope was formally renarrowed. The abandoned keypoint-analysis path was removed
in its entirety, \code{game\_control} was reworked from a game-input structure
into a general sensor-fusion payload, auto-calibration and normalized arm signals
were added, heart rate was mapped to \code{exertion\_level} and
\code{intensity\_zone}, and flat \code{signal\_log} records were introduced for
analysis. The documentation was rewritten around the new deliverable.

The pivot report recorded five risks. Reviewed at the close of the internship,
four materialized in some form: the detector did need substantial geometric
gating; IMU mounting did introduce an orientation offset requiring an
excursion-based workaround; the Garmin path did require a bridge workaround; and
camera-derived signals did require careful validation. The fifth --- avoiding any
implication that the game is complete --- was addressed by scope discipline.

\subsection{Week 7: inertial bring-up (June 29 -- July 5)}

The ESP32-S3 and BNO08x were assembled and validated. The I\textsuperscript{2}C
scanner confirmed the sensor at address \code{0x4B}; firmware was written to
publish structured telemetry; and both the USB serial path and the Wi-Fi UDP path
were confirmed against the Python bridge. Five capture sessions were recorded in
this period --- still, tilt sweep, and motion burst conditions --- and summarized
through the analysis pipeline. Those summaries recorded an effective sample rate
of approximately 15\,Hz and pose confidence medians above 0.84, confirming the
data was usable.

The honest assessment recorded at the time was that the breadboard assembly was
functional but too fragile for movement capture, which set the agenda for the
enclosure work.

\subsection{Week 8: wearable integration (July 6 -- July 12)}

The transport layer was unified so that a single launcher command listens on USB
serial and Wi-Fi simultaneously, removing the need to choose a transport before
each run. The Garmin BLE heart-rate bridge was added, the wearable JSON contract
was extended to carry heart-rate contact, resting and maximum references, and
optional RR intervals, and the offline analysis was extended to summarize
wearable status, heart-rate percentiles, exertion, and intensity zones.

\subsection{Week 9: consolidation (July 13 -- July 19)}

The Connect IQ watch application was developed, which required generating a
developer key and working through a sideloading process that initially produced
an on-device crash. Diagnosis identified a timer started from the application's
\code{onStart} lifecycle hook; moving it to the view lifecycle resolved it. The
Cloudflare Worker relay was written and deployed, replacing the ephemeral tunnel
approach. The enclosure was modelled in Blender.

\subsection{Week 10: application (July 20 -- July 26)}

This was the highest-output week. The enclosure was printed and validated; the
browser client was built from an early prototype into a client with a prescribed
exercise flow, a 3D avatar, and a real results screen; the exercise library was
expanded to ten; and the ESP32 Wi-Fi telemetry and Garmin bridges were hardened.
The second case revision was produced in response to fit observations from the
first print. A VPRI lab visit occurred on July 20, for which a project
introduction was prepared.

\subsection{Week 11: correctness (July 27 -- August 3)}

Two full real-hardware sessions were run with all three sensing modalities
operating together. These exposed failures that isolated testing had not: brief
dumbbell occlusions invalidating the load, a single stale sensor freezing
repetition counting, and an adaptive threshold permanently skewed by an early
outlier repetition. Each was diagnosed and corrected, and the validation
architecture described in Section~\ref{sec:browser} is the result. Per-exercise
validation was made genuinely distinct rather than several exercises sharing
identical detection logic, and a debug view was added.

The pattern worth recording is that every significant correctness fix in this
project originated in a real session, not in testing. The automated suite is
valuable for preventing regressions; it did not find these bugs.

\subsection{Week 12: release (August 4 -- August 7)}

Repository consolidation and final reporting, described in
Section~\ref{sec:consolidation}.

\section{Final Repository Consolidation}
\label{sec:consolidation}

Reproducibility is a research obligation, so the final week was spent making the
repository something a reader can actually use. The audit examined tracked files,
cross-file references, and --- importantly --- content excluded by version
control, which turned out to be where the significant problems were.

\subsection{Removed}

Superseded or unreferenced material was removed: a standalone training launcher
duplicating logic already in the command-line interface; an unreferenced dataset
preparation script; a synthetic heart-rate generator and its sample file; a
one-off workspace organizer; a legacy regression fixture and its serving route;
the serial-only firmware variant; a deprecated watch application and its
sideloading smoke test; a Blender autosave and a superseded G-code revision; and
four presentation iterations totalling approximately 14\,MB. Tracked files fell
from 124 to 83, then rose to 96 once the evidence figures described below were
brought under version control.

\subsection{Unified}

Two commands defined approximately fifty command-line options twice in nearly
identical blocks; they now share one definition and differ only through an
explicit defaults table, which also documents the differences. A 100-line
compatibility class whose only real output was pose liveness was folded into the
keypoint module. Dead browser code and unused style rules were removed.

\subsection{A defect found during unification}

The presentation module carried its own copy of the inertial intensity formula,
written before a raw angular-velocity term was added to the runtime version. The
developer monitor therefore under-reported real movement: a hand rotating at
approximately 68\,deg/s read 0.025 --- effectively ``steady'' --- on the monitor
while the runtime and the browser both read 0.617, or ``active''. The monitor now
imports the runtime function, so the two surfaces cannot diverge again.

A second latent defect surfaced in the same pass: one command's serial port
defaulted to a value that silently disabled the serial transport entirely, making
an explicitly requested serial connection a no-op.

Both defects share a cause worth naming: duplicated logic that drifted when only
one copy was updated. Neither was detectable by the test suite, because both
copies were individually self-consistent.

\subsection{Evidence preserved}

Training output is excluded from version control because a run is hundreds of
megabytes. That exclusion also captured the confusion matrix, precision-recall
curves, and per-epoch metrics for the detector --- the exact material this report
cites --- which existed on a single laptop with no backup. That subset is now
committed, with a document recording which run produced it and what the numbers
mean. Five capture sessions were similarly reduced from 54\,MB of video and
frames to 5.6\,KB of analysis summaries before the raw media was discarded.

Finally, the relay worker's source was committed but its deployment configuration
was not, meaning the service could be read but not rebuilt from the repository. A
deployment configuration was added.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Stated plainly, because a research artefact that overstates itself is worse than
a modest one.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2cm,style=nextline,itemsep=4pt]
\item[Single-camera depth] Body posture is estimated from one 2D camera. Depth is
approximate and derived from apparent scale, not measured.

\item[Detector false positives] As quantified in Section~\ref{sec:results}, the
detector proposes dumbbells in background regions at a high rate. System
behaviour depends on geometric gating, not on the detector alone.

\item[Single-side inertial verification] With one glove on one wrist by design,
grip-rotation differences on the uninstrumented arm cannot be verified by any
current sensor and continue to count from the camera's elbow-bend signal alone.

\item[Mounting offset] The case rotation on the strap sets an absolute pitch
offset. Absolute forearm elevation is therefore a best-effort convenience value;
validation deliberately relies on excursion instead.

\item[Wearable latency] Heart rate is a context and intensity signal, not a
control signal or a safety stop, and its samples can arrive with latency.

\item[No user study] The system has been validated technically and exercised in
real sessions by its developer. It has not been evaluated with independent
participants, so no claim is made about usability, engagement, or adherence.

\item[Calibration assumes cooperation] The calibration window assumes the user
moves through a genuine range. Quality reporting detects when this does not
happen, but cannot repair the resulting session.

\item[Proof-of-concept application] The browser client demonstrates the sensing
path. It is not a fitness product and should not be presented as one.
\end{description}

%==============================================================================
\section{Future Work}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Participant evaluation.} The most valuable next step by a wide
margin. The calibration mechanism specifically claims cross-user
transferability, and that claim is currently argued from design rather than
demonstrated with data.

\item \textbf{Local detector training data.} The detector was trained on general
annotated sources. Images from the actual lab setting --- the specific weights,
camera angle, lighting, and occlusion cases --- would directly address the
background false-positive rate.

\item \textbf{Tempo as a first-class signal.} Frame and sample timing are already
logged; surfacing tempo would complete the original proposal's signal triad of
repetition, range, and tempo.

\item \textbf{Longitudinal adherence analysis.} The frame-level export format was
designed for this and has not yet been used across sessions over time.

\item \textbf{Bilateral inertial sensing.} A second glove would remove the
single-side verification limitation, at the cost of the asymmetric-hardware
property the current contract deliberately handles.

\item \textbf{Rehabilitation framing.} The signal set --- range utilization,
bilateral symmetry, stability, exertion --- maps naturally onto rehabilitation
assessment. This is the most promising direction beyond fitness.
\end{enumerate}

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

The internship delivered a working sensor-fusion engine that converts camera,
inertial, and physiological measurements into a single documented, per-user
calibrated signal contract, together with a browser application that consumes
that contract to prescribe exercises, validate repetitions against multi-sensor
evidence, and provide live feedback.

The decision that made this possible was narrowing scope in week six. The
original proposal contained three large deliverables whose dependencies ran in
series; attempting all three would have produced three unfinished halves. Naming
the middle layer as the contribution, freezing the vision scope, and reducing the
game to a proof of concept converted an unachievable plan into a completed one.

Three findings are worth carrying forward beyond this project. First, aggregate
detector metrics can materially overstate usable performance: the gap between
mAP@50 of 0.919 and a background false-positive rate of 0.72 is the difference
between a detector that scores well and one that can drive an interaction
unaided. Second, in a multi-sensor system, allowing every sensor to veto produces
worse behaviour than treating most sensors as corroboration --- a single stale
input should degrade confidence, not halt the system. Third, every significant
correctness defect in this project was found in a real session with all hardware
present, and none were found by an automated suite that nonetheless remains
essential for preventing their return.
